    4The shield

    The Sender holds public RaylsERC20 (the issuer minted them to the Sender's account) and deposits all of it into the vault, which turns it into the Sender's first note: one leaf of the vault's Merkle tree. The deposit is one transaction, Erc20CoinVault.depositV2([amount, pkSpend, salt, tokenId], ciphertextI, ciphertextII), sent from the Sender's own account.

    The field every value lives in

    𝔽p={0,1,…,p−1},a+b,a·bmodp,−1≡p−1

    A note's four values, its commitment, every node of the tree, the keys and the nullifiers are all elements of 𝔽p, the scalar field of the BN254 curve: the integers modulo the -bit prime

    p

    The Groth16 proofs of the later steps are made over BN254, so a circuit's wires carry exactly these numbers and its constraints are equations mod p. The arithmetic wraps around: in the field, −1 is p − 1, a valid and enormous amount. That is why the payment's circuit checks that every amount is small (the range check) instead of trusting that none is negative.

    The vault does not take a commitment from the depositor: it computes C on chain, in depositV2, from the four values in the calldata, after pulling amount tokens from msg.sender. So the note it inserts is bound to the tokens it actually received; a deposit cannot mint a note worth more than it paid. With this run's calldata:

    What a commitment hides, and what this one does not

    A commitment published on its own is hiding: from C nobody learns the amount or the owner. Two properties give that. Poseidon is one-way, so C cannot be run backwards to its inputs. And the salt is random: without it, C would be Poseidon(pk_spend, amount, token), and anyone could hash the owner's public key with amount = 1, 2, 3, … until one matched. With a salt drawn from the field, each guessed amount must come with a guessed salt, one chance in p ≈ 2254. It is also binding: Poseidon is collision-resistant, so no one can find a second opening of C with another amount.

    (ss,c)=ML-KEM-768.Encaps(ekviewowner) salt=HKDF-SHA256(ss,info=“note salt”)modp

    The salt is not drawn and remembered: it is derived from a fresh ML-KEM secret shared with the note's owner, so the owner can derive it again from the chain (step 9 shows how a note sealed this way is found and opened). Here the owner is the Sender, and c is ciphertextI.

    This note hides nothing. Its whole opening, pk_spend, salt, amount and token, is calldata, and pk_spend is the key the Sender registered. Anyone can recompute C and see that the Sender's account put into a note the Sender owns. That is the price of entering: the vault has to see the amount it is paid. Privacy starts at the first spend, when the Sender proves they own some leaf of the tree without saying which, and the new notes' openings never touch the chain.

    Poseidon, as a sponge

    absorbs=(0,x1,…,xn)∈𝔽pt,t=n+1 permutes←π(s),π=RF/2full∘RPpartial∘RF/2full rounds squeezePoseidon(x1,…,xn)=s0 rounds←M·S(s+cr),S:x↦x5

    The state is t field elements: one capacity lane, which starts at 0 and is never input, and n rate lanes that take the inputs. Every hash here absorbs its inputs in one block, applies the permutation π once and squeezes one element. A round adds round constants cr, applies the S-box x ↦ x⁵ (to every lane in a full round, to one lane in a partial round) and mixes the lanes with a t × t MDS matrix M. x⁵ is a permutation of the field because gcd(5, p − 1) = 1, which the run checked. The deployed parameters are circomlib's, which target 128-bit security; the rounds themselves are a black box here.

    RF RP,t=2 · pk_spend = Poseidon(sk_spend) RP,t=3 · each node of the Merkle tree RP,t=5 · the note commitment C

    Poseidon is built for this field: its S-box costs three multiplications, so a hash is a few hundred constraints in a circuit, where SHA-256 would cost tens of thousands. One-wayness, collision resistance and the hiding above are assumptions about π, backed by public cryptanalysis rather than a reduction to a classical hard problem.

    The crowd. Which leaf a spend opens is hidden among the leaves of its tree, so a tree's 65,536 slots bound the anonymity set. In fact the bound is 65,535: the vault opens a new tree when a batch of leaves would reach the 65,536th slot, and a new tree's crowd starts again from one.     What the chain now holds

    The ledger adds the deposit: its msg.sender, its calldata in full (the opening, and the note's ciphertexts, which step 9 explains) and the leaf the Commitment event inserted. The mint and the approve before it are public transactions too, with the same account and amount; so are the token's Transfer events.

    msg.sender names the Sender's account as the depositor. For a shield that link is not the leak, since pk_spend in the calldata names the Sender too. It matters on the payment, where the full protocol lets a relayer send the transaction for a fee.

    5The tag channel

Each later payment on the tag channel can carry a tag derived from ss, which only Ana recognises. It is one transaction, TagChannelRegistry.openChannel(c1, c2, bitmap), sent from the Sender's own account.

    6The payment

    The Sender pays Ana of the note and keeps as change. It goes over the tag channel of step 5, in one transaction, EnygmaDvp.channelPayment(receipt, vaultId, blockNumber, tag, ctxt), sent from the Sender's own account. The receipt holds one Groth16 proof π of the Payment circuit and the proof's eight public inputs. blockNumber, tag and ctxt are Ana's: the block b the payment is for, a tag only she recognises, and her note's opening, sealed (step 8). Everything else about the spend is the witness, and it stays in the Sender's wallet.

    The circuit

    The witness (teal, left) is what the Sender knows: the input note's opening and sk_spend, its Merkle path, and the two outputs' openings. It enters the circuit and never leaves it. Each gate is one of the circuit's constraints, where H is Poseidon: equations mod p that the witness must satisfy, all together. Only the eight public inputs (indigo, right) leave, to the Verifier. The proof π says that a witness satisfying every gate exists for these public inputs, and it says nothing more about that witness. Step 7, the proof, shows how. Point at a gate, or at a card below, to light up its wires.

    Every gate, and what it stops

    G1Message is 0 or 1

    Stops a receipt being settled as the other kind. A payment carries Message 0 and a withdrawal carries 1, and each settlement function takes only its own value, so a withdrawal can't be settled as a payment or the other way round. payment() requires 0.

    G2The range check

    0≤v<1036forv∈{,,}

    1036 = . The bound is a comparison on the value's bits inside the circuit.

    Stops minting through a "negative" change. In the field, −1 is p − 1. Without this gate, a 100 note could pay out 101 and p − 1: the sum is 100 mod p, so conservation would hold. That is a 101 note minted from a 100 note, and the change note would be worth a near-infinite amount. Three values under 1036 add up to far less than p, so the sums can't wrap.

    G3Ownership

    pkin=Poseidon(skin)=

    pk_in is not part of the witness. The circuit derives it from sk_in, and it is the Sender's registered pk_spend.

    Stops spending someone else's note. A leaf opens only under a pk_in the prover computed from its secret, and pk_spend is public (registration publishes it) while sk_spend can't be recovered from it. Knowing a note's owner and opening is not enough to spend it.

    G4The zero-value gating, enable

    z=isZero(vin),enable=1−z= enable·(R−root)=0z·R=0 enable·(N−nf)=0z·N=0

    R and N are the public root and nullifier. root and nf are the values the circuit computes (G6, G7). A zero-value input is padding, so it needs no real note and its path and nullifier are not checked. This input is real: enable = 1, so R and N must be the computed ones.

    Stops the on-chain checks being bypassed (VULN-3). The vault checks isValidRoot and spends a nullifier only for an input whose root is not 0. A zero-value input must therefore publish root 0 and nullifier 0, so that the vault skips exactly the inputs the circuit skipped. Without z·R = 0 and z·N = 0, a padding input could publish any valid root and a nullifier that no constraint computed. The vault would pass the root check and record that nullifier as spent. If it were another user's, their note would be burned.

    7The proof

    The payment's receipt held one Groth16 proof π = (A, B, C): two points of BN254's group G1 and one of G2, 256 bytes whatever the circuit's size. It convinced the Verifier that a witness satisfying every gate of step 6 exists, and said nothing more. This step opens it up: how the gates become one polynomial identity, what the setup and the prover computed, and the one equation the chain checked. Verifying reads only, so the ledger is unchanged: it already holds π and the public inputs.

    From gates to R1CS

    ⟨ai,z⟩·⟨bi,z⟩=⟨ci,z⟩for each constrainti,z=(1,x1,…,x7,w1,…)∈𝔽pm

    The circuit compiles every gate of step 6 to rank-1 constraints. Each one says that two linear combinations of the wires, multiplied, equal a third. A Poseidon S-box x⁵ takes three of them, and a range check takes several per bit: it splits the value into bits and compares them one by one. z holds every wire: the constant 1, the eight public inputs xi, and the private wires, which are step 6's witness and every intermediate value it implies. With this run's keys:

    m==1+public+private

    From R1CS to a QAP

    uj(ωi)=ai,j,vj(ωi)=bi,j,wj(ωi)=ci,j (∑zjuj(X))·(∑zjvj(X))−∑zjwj(X)=h(X)·t(X) t(X)=Xn−1=∏i=0n−1(X−ωi) n=2=,<constraints≤n ω=

    Each constraint gets one point ωi of the set H = {1, ω, …, ωn−1}. Each column j of the three matrices becomes a polynomial through that column's entries at those points. A z satisfies every constraint exactly when the left side vanishes at every point of H, which is when t(X), the polynomial vanishing on H, divides it. So a quotient h(X) exists, and thousands of constraints have become one identity of polynomials. gnark makes H the smallest power-of-two set that holds every constraint, so that FFTs can move a polynomial between its values and its coefficients. ω is a primitive n-th root of unity in 𝔽p; the run checked that ωn = 1 and ωn/2 ≠ 1. Checking the identity at one secret random point τ is enough: two different polynomials of degree under 2n agree on at most 2n points, so a false one survives with probability about 2n/p (the Schwartz–Zippel lemma).

    The setup: τ, and the toxic waste

    τ,α,β,γ,δ←$𝔽p,[x]1=x·G1,[x]2=x·G2 verifying key:[α]1,[β]2,[γ]2,[δ]2,ICj=[βuj(τ)+αvj(τ)+wj(τ)γ]1forj≤7 proving key:[uj(τ)]1,[vj(τ)]1,2,[βuj(τ)+αvj(τ)+wj(τ)δ]1forj>7,[τit(τ)δ]1,…

    G1 and G2 are BN254's two groups of prime order p, with generators G1 and G2. The setup evaluates every polynomial at τ inside the group: the keys hold multiples of the generators, never τ itself, and recovering τ from them would mean solving a discrete logarithm. The public wires' combinations, divided by γ, go to the verifying key as IC0 … IC7. The private wires' combinations, divided by δ, go to the proving key, which the run read: its α, β and δ are the verifying key's, and it has a Z point for each of n − 1 powers of τ.

    A G1 point is shown as (x, y) over the base field 𝔽q. A G2 point's coordinates lie in 𝔽q² = 𝔽q[u]/(u² + 1), each written re + im·u.

    This deployment's setup is single-party. Its keys come from one call to gnark's groth16.Setup (gnark_circuits/scripts/setup.go), on one machine. That call drew τ, α, β, γ and δ and discarded them, but nothing proves it did. Whoever ran it could have kept them, the toxic waste, and with them made a valid proof for any public inputs: a nullifier no note derives, an output worth more than its input. No verifier could tell. Production needs a multi-party ceremony, where each participant mixes a secret of their own into the keys, so the final secrets combine all of theirs. Then the keys are sound if one honest participant destroyed theirs.

    What the proof guarantees, and on what

    Knowledge soundness. A prover who convinces the Verifier knows a witness that satisfies every gate, so every Stops line of step 6 holds. Groth16's proof of this is in the generic group model, and it assumes that nobody holds the setup's secrets. It also rests on BN254's discrete logarithms being hard. After the 2016 improvements to the number field sieve, they are estimated at about 100 bits of security, short of the 128 the curve was chosen for.

    Zero-knowledge. π reveals nothing about the witness beyond the statement being true, and this holds perfectly, against an adversary of any power. r and s make A and B uniformly random, and the equation then fixes C, so π's distribution is the same whatever witness made it. The proof of this is a simulator that holds the setup's secrets and makes proofs with no witness at all, which is also why those secrets must be gone. It assumes the keys were formed honestly.

    Malleability. Anyone can turn π into a different valid proof of the same x, without knowing the witness. So nothing on chain is keyed by π. The vault spends the nullifier, which the proof binds.

    What the scan reveals, and what it costs

    The scan costs one Poseidon per tag channel per block and one read per block, so it grows with Ana's tag channels and the blocks she covers, not with other people's payments.     What the secrecy rests on: module-LWE

    t=As+e,A∈Rq3×3,s,e∈Rq3small,Rq=ℤq[X]/(X256+1),q=3329

    Ana's note is as secret as ss, and ss crossed the chain once, in the tag channel's capsule c1 (step 5), an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation to her ek_view. ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) is IND-CCA2 secure if module-LWE is hard: given A and t, telling t from random, or finding s, is hard when s and e are small. Its public key ek is such a t (here t is the lattice vector, not a tag), with the seed ρ that A is expanded from, and 768 is the dimension, 3 × 256. Module-LWE is a lattice problem. No algorithm is known to solve it efficiently, on a classical computer or on a quantum one. Shor's algorithm, which breaks RSA and elliptic-curve key exchange, does not apply to it. ML-KEM-768 targets NIST's security category 3, as hard to break as AES-192. HKDF-SHA256 and AES-256 are symmetric, and Grover's search only halves their key strength, which leaves AES-256 at about 128 bits. So the note's encryption is post-quantum. That matters because ciphertexts on a chain are permanent: one recorded today can be attacked by any later computer.

    This claim covers the encryption only. The Groth16 proofs live on BN254, whose security rests on discrete logarithms, which Shor's algorithm does break. A quantum adversary could forge a proof, though not read a past note. The tag is a Poseidon hash over ss mod p; finding ss from it is a preimage search, which Grover's algorithm only speeds up quadratically.

Every transaction also shows its gas, its nonce and its block time, and the token transfers around the shield and the withdrawal repeat their public amounts. The spent note can be any leaf under the root shown, which is never more than 65,535 notes and can be far fewer on a quiet vault or in a tree that has just started. With so few users and notes, as in this story, timing and the public amounts make a guess easy, and a good guess needs no cryptography. The UserRegistry also lists everyone who could be a payee.     What the full protocol adds

    The relayer. Here the amber rows are the one direct link that remains: each spender sent their own transaction. In the full protocol a relayer sends it from its own account, so msg.sender is the relayer's and no longer names who spent. That is what the relayer buys, at the cost of a fee.
    The USDr fee. On a payment the relayer is paid in USDr, so that a user never has to appear on chain to pay it. (A withdrawal through a relayer is paid from the note instead: see step 10.) A second proof, UsdrFee, spends a USDr note into the relayer's fee note beside the payment. The fee is a public input of that proof, so it is one more public amount.
    Tag channels. A relayer hides who sent a transaction, not what its tags carry. Without one a payee only tries every note, and once a relayer hides msg.sender the auditor has no name for a payer. Step 9 shows what a tag channel buys the payee, and step 11 what it gives the auditor. The proof binds the tag (step 8), so a relayer can't strip it.
