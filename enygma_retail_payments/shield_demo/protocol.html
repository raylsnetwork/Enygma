    4The shield

    The Sender holds public RaylsERC20 (the issuer minted them to the Sender's account) and deposits all of it into the vault, which turns it into the Sender's first note: one leaf of the vault's Merkle tree. The deposit is one transaction, Erc20CoinVault.depositV2([amount, pkSpend, salt, tokenId], ciphertextI, ciphertextII), sent from the Sender's own account.

    The field every value lives in

    𝔽p={0,1,…,p−1},a+b,a·bmodp,−1≡p−1

    A note's four values, its commitment, every node of the tree, the keys and the nullifiers are all elements of 𝔽p, the scalar field of the BN254 curve: the integers modulo the -bit prime

    p

    The Groth16 proofs of the later steps are made over BN254, so a circuit's wires carry exactly these numbers and its constraints are equations mod p. The arithmetic wraps around: in the field, −1 is p − 1, a valid and enormous amount. That is why the payment's circuit checks that every amount is small (the range check) instead of trusting that none is negative.

    The note commitment, computed by the vault

    The vault does not take a commitment from the depositor: it computes C on chain, in depositV2, from the four values in the calldata, after pulling amount tokens from msg.sender. So the note it inserts is bound to the tokens it actually received; a deposit cannot mint a note worth more than it paid. With this run's calldata:

    What a commitment hides, and what this one does not

    A commitment published on its own is hiding: from C nobody learns the amount or the owner. Two properties give that. Poseidon is one-way, so C cannot be run backwards to its inputs. And the salt is random: without it, C would be Poseidon(pk_spend, amount, token), and anyone could hash the owner's public key with amount = 1, 2, 3, … until one matched. With a salt drawn from the field, each guessed amount must come with a guessed salt, one chance in p ≈ 2254. It is also binding: Poseidon is collision-resistant, so no one can find a second opening of C with another amount.

    (ss,c)=ML-KEM-768.Encaps(ekviewowner) salt=HKDF-SHA256(ss,info=“note salt”)modp

    The salt is not drawn and remembered: it is derived from a fresh ML-KEM secret shared with the note's owner, so the owner can derive it again from the chain (Ana's discovery, step 7, opens a note this way). Here the owner is the Sender, and c is ciphertextI.

    This note hides nothing. Its whole opening, pk_spend, salt, amount and token, is calldata, and pk_spend is the key the Sender registered. Anyone can recompute C and see that the Sender's account put into a note the Sender owns. That is the price of entering: the vault has to see the amount it is paid. Privacy starts at the first spend, when the Sender proves they own some leaf of the tree without saying which, and the new notes' openings never touch the chain.

    Poseidon, as a sponge

    absorbs=(0,x1,…,xn)∈𝔽pt,t=n+1 permutes←π(s),π=RF/2full∘RPpartial∘RF/2full rounds squeezePoseidon(x1,…,xn)=s0 rounds←M·S(s+cr),S:x↦x5

    The state is t field elements: one capacity lane, which starts at 0 and is never input, and n rate lanes that take the inputs. Every hash here absorbs its inputs in one block, applies the permutation π once and squeezes one element. A round adds round constants cr, applies the S-box x ↦ x⁵ (to every lane in a full round, to one lane in a partial round) and mixes the lanes with a t × t MDS matrix M. x⁵ is a permutation of the field because gcd(5, p − 1) = 1, which the run checked. The deployed parameters are circomlib's, which target 128-bit security; the rounds themselves are a black box here.

    RF RP,t=2 · pk_spend = Poseidon(sk_spend) RP,t=3 · each node of the Merkle tree RP,t=5 · the note commitment C

    Poseidon is built for this field: its S-box costs three multiplications, so a hash is a few hundred constraints in a circuit, where SHA-256 would cost tens of thousands. One-wayness, collision resistance and the hiding above are assumptions about π, backed by public cryptanalysis rather than a reduction to a classical hard problem.

    The vault keeps its notes in a binary Merkle tree of depth 8, so 2⁸ = 256 leaf slots. Inserting C rehashes the path from its slot to the root, and the vault records the new root in its history: isValidRoot(tree, root) answers whether a root is one the tree has had.     The crowd. Which leaf a spend opens is hidden among the leaves of its tree, so a tree's 256 slots bound the anonymity set. In fact the bound is 255: the vault opens a new tree when a batch of leaves would reach the 256th slot, and a new tree's crowd starts again from one. This tree holds leaves. A spent note stays in the crowd, since its nullifier does not say which leaf it was.

    What the chain now holds

    The ledger adds the deposit: its msg.sender, its calldata in full (the opening, and the note's ciphertexts, which step 7 explains with Ana's note) and the leaf the Commitment event inserted. The mint and the approve before it are public transactions too, with the same account and amount; so are the token's Transfer events.

    msg.sender names the Sender's account as the depositor. For a shield that link is not the leak, since pk_spend in the calldata names the Sender too. It matters on the payment, where the full protocol lets a relayer send the transaction for a fee.

    The circuit

    The witness (teal, left) is what the Sender knows: the input note's opening and sk_spend, its Merkle path, and the two outputs' openings. It enters the circuit and never leaves it. Each gate is one of the circuit's constraints, where H is Poseidon: equations mod p that the witness must satisfy, all together. Only the seven public inputs (indigo, right) leave, to the Verifier. The proof π says that a witness satisfying every gate exists for these public inputs, and it says nothing more about that witness. Step 6, the proof, shows how. Point at a gate, or at a card below, to light up its wires.

    Stops minting through a "negative" change. In the field, −1 is p − 1. Without this gate, a 100 note could pay out 101 and p − 1: the sum is 100 mod p, so conservation would hold. That is a 101 note minted from a 100 note, and the change note would be worth a near-infinite amount. Three values under 1036 add up to far less than p, so the sums can't wrap.

    R and N are the public root and nullifier. root and nf are the values the circuit computes (G6, G7). A zero-value input is padding, so it needs no real note and its path and nullifier are not checked. This input is real: enable = 1, so R and N must be the computed ones.

    6The proof

    The payment's receipt held one Groth16 proof π = (A, B, C): two points of BN254's group G1 and one of G2, 256 bytes whatever the circuit's size. It convinced the Verifier that a witness satisfying every gate of step 5 exists, and said nothing more. This step opens it up: how the gates become one polynomial identity, what the setup and the prover computed, and the one equation the chain checked. Verifying reads only, so the ledger is unchanged: it already holds π and the public inputs.

    From gates to R1CS

    ⟨ai,z⟩·⟨bi,z⟩=⟨ci,z⟩for each constrainti,z=(1,x1,…,x7,w1,…)∈𝔽pm

    The circuit compiles every gate of step 5 to rank-1 constraints. Each one says that two linear combinations of the wires, multiplied, equal a third. A Poseidon S-box x⁵ takes three of them, and a range check takes several per bit: it splits the value into bits and compares them one by one. z holds every wire: the constant 1, the seven public inputs xi, and the private wires, which are step 5's witness and every intermediate value it implies. With this run's keys:

    m==1+public+private

    From R1CS to a QAP

    uj(ωi)=ai,j,vj(ωi)=bi,j,wj(ωi)=ci,j (∑zjuj(X))·(∑zjvj(X))−∑zjwj(X)=h(X)·t(X) t(X)=Xn−1=∏i=0n−1(X−ωi) n=2=,<constraints≤n ω=

    Each constraint gets one point ωi of the set H = {1, ω, …, ωn−1}. Each column j of the three matrices becomes a polynomial through that column's entries at those points. A z satisfies every constraint exactly when the left side vanishes at every point of H, which is when t(X), the polynomial vanishing on H, divides it. So a quotient h(X) exists, and thousands of constraints have become one identity of polynomials. gnark makes H the smallest power-of-two set that holds every constraint, so that FFTs can move a polynomial between its values and its coefficients. ω is a primitive n-th root of unity in 𝔽p; the run checked that ωn = 1 and ωn/2 ≠ 1. Checking the identity at one secret random point τ is enough: two different polynomials of degree under 2n agree on at most 2n points, so a false one survives with probability about 2n/p (the Schwartz–Zippel lemma).

    The setup: τ, and the toxic waste

    τ,α,β,γ,δ←$𝔽p,[x]1=x·G1,[x]2=x·G2 verifying key:[α]1,[β]2,[γ]2,[δ]2,ICj=[βuj(τ)+αvj(τ)+wj(τ)γ]1forj≤7 proving key:[uj(τ)]1,[vj(τ)]1,2,[βuj(τ)+αvj(τ)+wj(τ)δ]1forj>7,[τit(τ)δ]1,…

    G1 and G2 are BN254's two groups of prime order p, with generators G1 and G2. The setup evaluates every polynomial at τ inside the group: the keys hold multiples of the generators, never τ itself, and recovering τ from them would mean solving a discrete logarithm. The public wires' combinations, divided by γ, go to the verifying key as IC0 … IC7. The private wires' combinations, divided by δ, go to the proving key, which the run read: its α, β and δ are the verifying key's, and it has a Z point for each of n − 1 powers of τ.

    Verifying key · slot 0 of the Verifier [α]1 [β]2 [γ]2 [δ]2 IC0 IC1 IC2 IC3 IC4 IC5 IC6 IC7

    A G1 point is shown as (x, y) over the base field 𝔽q. A G2 point's coordinates lie in 𝔽q² = 𝔽q[u]/(u² + 1), each written re + im·u.

    This deployment's setup is single-party. Its keys come from one call to gnark's groth16.Setup (gnark_circuits/scripts/setup.go), on one machine. That call drew τ, α, β, γ and δ and discarded them, but nothing proves it did. Whoever ran it could have kept them, the toxic waste, and with them made a valid proof for any public inputs: a nullifier no note derives, an output worth more than its input. No verifier could tell. Production needs a multi-party ceremony, where each participant mixes a secret of their own into the keys, so the final secrets combine all of theirs. Then the keys are sound if one honest participant destroyed theirs.

    A=[a]1,a=α+∑zjuj(τ)+rδ B=[b]2,b=β+∑zjvj(τ)+sδ C=[∑j>7zj(βuj+αvj+wj)(τ)+h(τ)t(τ)δ+sa+rb−rsδ]1

    The prover never sees τ. It computes h(X) by dividing by t(X), with FFTs on a coset of H: t vanishes on H itself, so the division is done off it. Then it builds A, B and C as sums of the proving key's points, weighted by the wires z and by h's coefficients. r and s are fresh random scalars for each proof, and they make π zero-knowledge. The prover draws them, and they appear nowhere in π. a and b are A's and B's scalars, which nobody knows as numbers: the formula for C is what its sum of points comes to.

    What the proof guarantees, and on what

    Knowledge soundness. A prover who convinces the Verifier knows a witness that satisfies every gate, so every Stops line of step 5 holds. Groth16's proof of this is in the generic group model, and it assumes that nobody holds the setup's secrets. It also rests on BN254's discrete logarithms being hard. After the 2016 improvements to the number field sieve, they are estimated at about 100 bits of security, short of the 128 the curve was chosen for.

    Zero-knowledge. π reveals nothing about the witness beyond the statement being true, and this holds perfectly, against an adversary of any power. r and s make A and B uniformly random, and the equation then fixes C, so π's distribution is the same whatever witness made it. The proof of this is a simulator that holds the setup's secrets and makes proofs with no witness at all, which is also why those secrets must be gone. It assumes the keys were formed honestly.

    Malleability. Anyone can turn π into a different valid proof of the same x, without knowing the witness. So nothing on chain is keyed by π. The vault spends the nullifier, which the proof binds.

    What the chain now holds

    Nothing new. verifyProof is a view: the vault called it inside the payment's transaction, and the run called it again to read its answer. The ledger already lists π and x with the payment.

    What the encryption rests on: module-LWE

    t=As+e,A∈Rq3×3,s,e∈Rq3small,Rq=ℤq[X]/(X256+1),q=3329

    ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) is IND-CCA2 secure if module-LWE is hard: given A and t, telling t from random, or finding s, is hard when s and e are small. Its public key ek is such a t, with the seed ρ that A is expanded from, and 768 is the dimension, 3 × 256. Module-LWE is a lattice problem. No algorithm is known to solve it efficiently, on a classical computer or on a quantum one. Shor's algorithm, which breaks RSA and elliptic-curve key exchange, does not apply to it. ML-KEM-768 targets NIST's security category 3, as hard to break as AES-192. HKDF-SHA256 and AES-256 are symmetric, and Grover's search only halves their key strength, which leaves AES-256 at about 128 bits. So a note's encryption is post-quantum. That matters because ciphertexts on a chain are permanent: one recorded today can be attacked by any later computer.

    This claim covers the encryption only. The Groth16 proofs live on BN254, whose security rests on discrete logarithms, which Shor's algorithm does break. A quantum adversary could forge a proof, though not read a past note.

    Ana's scan: trial decryption

    for each event(Ce,ce,noncee‖cte) sse=ML-KEM-768.Decaps(dkviewAna,ce) ke=HKDF-SHA256(sse,info=“encryption key”) me=AES-256-GCM.Openke(noncee,cte)=(token,amount)or⊥ this runofevents open, to(,)

    Decaps does not fail on a capsule made for someone else. ML-KEM rejects implicitly, so such a capsule decapsulates to an unrelated, pseudorandom sse. What fails is AES-GCM. For a payload sealed honestly under the Sender's k, the 16-byte tag checks under another key with probability 2−128, and Open returns ⊥. AES-GCM does not commit to its key, though, so a dishonest Sender could craft one payload that opens under two keys. The tag doesn't stop that, but Ana's opening check, below, does. A note that isn't Ana's gives the same ⊥ whoever it belongs to. The run that made these values tried Ana's key on all Payment events EnygmaDvp had emitted by then, and it opened : hers. The Sender's view key, tried on Ana's ciphertext, decapsulated to another secret and failed.

    Scanning reveals nothing about which notes are hers. The wallet fetches every Payment event, as every wallet does, and decrypts them locally. What it asks of the chain doesn't depend on whose notes they are or on what it finds, so the node it reads from learns nothing. The events don't name Ana either. Hiding which ek a capsule was made for is a property of its own, key privacy, apart from the secrecy of ss. It has been studied for Kyber, ML-KEM's predecessor, but FIPS 203 does not promise it.

    An AES-GCM tag shows only that the payload was sealed under k, by someone who knows ss, which here means the Sender. It doesn't show that the payload matches the note. π binds C0 but not the ciphertexts, so a Sender could seal "1000" beside a note worth 60. Ana's wallet therefore recomputes the commitment from her own pk_spend, the salt she derived and the decrypted amount and token. It keeps the note only if that equals the published C. Poseidon is binding, so no other amount opens C. The run tested this. It sealed a payload claiming under Ana's key, beside the same capsule. The payload opened, its opening hashed to another commitment, and the client library's scan refused it.

    In the client library (ScanForErc20Notes), a bad event stops the whole scan with an error, instead of being skipped. A mismatched opening does this to its recipient's scan. A capsule that is not 1088 bytes does it to every wallet's, because Decaps rejects its length, and neither payment() nor π checks ctxt. So any payer can stall every scan until wallets skip such events. The run checked both cases.

    Ana now holds her note's whole opening, and with her sk_spend she can spend it.
